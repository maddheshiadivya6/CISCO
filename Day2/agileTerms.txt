
============================================================ PART 1:
AGILE METHODOLOGY
============================================================

1.  WHAT IS AGILE?

Agile is an approach to developing software in small parts, collecting
feedback regularly, and improving the product based on what the team
learns.

Instead of waiting until the entire project is finished before showing
it to customers, the team delivers useful parts of the product step by
step.

Example: A company wants to build an online shopping application. It
needs login, product search, a shopping cart, payment, and order
tracking. An Agile team might build and test login first, then product
search, then the shopping cart, and continue improving the application
in small steps.

Main characteristics of Agile: - Small, frequent deliveries of useful
software. - Regular customer and stakeholder feedback. - Close
collaboration between team members. - Ability to respond to changing
requirements. - Continuous testing and quality improvement. - Regular
reflection on how the team can work better. - Focus on working software
and customer value.

Why use Agile? - Customers can see useful results earlier. - Problems
and misunderstandings can be discovered sooner. - The team can adjust
priorities when needs change. - Developers and business representatives
can communicate regularly. - The team can learn from feedback throughout
development.

Agile does not mean there is no planning, documentation, or deadlines.
It means planning and documentation are appropriate to the work and the
team can adapt when it receives new information.

2.  AGILE AND TRADITIONAL DEVELOPMENT

Traditional development often plans most of the project in advance and
may deliver working software near the end. Agile development plans and
delivers work in smaller cycles and collects feedback regularly.

Traditional approach: - Often plans a large amount of work before
implementation. - Customer feedback may arrive later. - Testing may be
concentrated toward the end. - Changes late in the project may be
expensive.

Agile approach: - Plans work in smaller cycles. - Collects feedback
throughout development. - Tests throughout development. - Uses new
information to adjust future work.

Neither approach is automatically right for every project. The best
approach depends on the project, risks, regulations, requirements, and
environment.

3.  THE FOUR VALUES OF THE AGILE MANIFESTO

1)  Individuals and interactions over processes and tools: Communication
    between people is especially important. Tools and processes are
    useful, but they should support collaboration rather than replace
    it.

2)  Working software over comprehensive documentation: A working product
    is especially valuable. Documentation is still useful when it helps
    people build, operate, or understand the product.

3)  Customer collaboration over contract negotiation: The team works
    with customers to understand needs and feedback, instead of relying
    only on the original agreement.

4)  Responding to change over following a plan: The team can adapt when
    new information shows that a different approach would deliver more
    value.

The word “over” does not mean the item on the right has no value. It
means the item on the left is valued more when the team must make a
trade-off.

4.  THE 12 AGILE PRINCIPLES IN SIMPLE ENGLISH

1)  Customer satisfaction: Deliver useful software early and regularly.

2)  Welcome changing requirements: Accept useful changes, even when
    development has already started.

3)  Frequent delivery: Deliver working software frequently, preferably
    in short cycles.

4)  Business people and developers collaborate: People who understand
    business needs and people who build the product communicate
    regularly.

5)  Trust motivated people: Give team members the support, resources,
    and environment needed to do good work.

6)  Direct communication: Clear, direct communication helps reduce
    misunderstanding.

7)  Working software measures progress: Progress should be demonstrated
    through useful software, not only reports or documents.

8)  Sustainable pace: The team should work at a pace it can maintain
    without constant exhaustion.

9)  Technical excellence: Good design, clean code, testing, and
    technical quality make future changes easier.

10) Simplicity: Avoid unnecessary work and complexity.

11) Self-organizing teams: Teams make appropriate decisions about how to
    organize their work.

12) Regular reflection: The team regularly reviews how it works and
    makes improvements.

Example: A team building a network-monitoring application learns that
critical alerts are more important to users than a planned dashboard
color change. The team can reconsider priorities so that more valuable
work is addressed sooner.

============================================================ PART 2:
SCRUM BASICS
============================================================

5.  WHAT IS SCRUM?

Scrum is a lightweight framework that helps people work together to
solve complex problems and deliver valuable products through short,
fixed-length cycles called Sprints.

Scrum provides: - Accountabilities (often informally called roles). -
Events for planning, inspection, feedback, and improvement. - Artifacts
that make work visible. - Commitments that help the team focus on goals
and quality.

Agile is the broader set of values and principles. Scrum is one
framework that can be used to apply Agile ideas.

6.  WHAT IS A SPRINT?

A Sprint is a fixed period of one month or less during which the Scrum
Team works toward a Sprint Goal and creates a valuable, usable
Increment.

A Sprint may last one, two, three, or four weeks. The team chooses a
suitable length and generally keeps it consistent.

During a Sprint: - The team conducts Sprint Planning. - Developers build
and test the product. - Developers hold a Daily Scrum every working
day. - The team conducts a Sprint Review. - The team conducts a Sprint
Retrospective. - The team works toward the Sprint Goal.

Important Sprint points: - A Sprint has a clear goal. - Quality must not
be reduced just to finish more work. - The plan can be adapted as the
team learns more. - Scope can be clarified and renegotiated with the
Product Owner as long as the Sprint Goal is not endangered. - A Sprint
lasts no longer than one month.

Example: Sprint Goal: “Provide basic visibility into network devices,
their health, and active alerts.” The team selects appropriate work,
develops and tests it, and inspects the result at the end of the Sprint.

7.  WHAT IS AN ITERATION?

An iteration is a repeated cycle of development work in which the team
plans, builds, tests, and learns.

The words “iteration” and “Sprint” are related but are not exactly
interchangeable in every methodology: - Scrum formally uses the term
Sprint. - Other development approaches may call a development cycle an
iteration. - Both concepts involve repeated cycles of work and learning.

Sprint versus iteration: - A Sprint is a defined Scrum concept with a
Sprint Goal and Scrum events. - An iteration is a general
development-cycle term whose structure depends on the methodology being
used.

============================================================ PART 3:
SCRUM ROLES AND RESPONSIBILITIES
============================================================

The three Scrum Team accountabilities are: 1. Product Owner. 2. Scrum
Master. 3. Developers.

Stakeholders also contribute valuable information and feedback, but they
are not a fourth formal Scrum Team accountability.

8.  PRODUCT OWNER (PO)

Main responsibility: Maximize the value of the product resulting from
the Scrum Team’s work.

The Product Owner helps the team understand what product work is most
valuable and ensures the Product Backlog is effectively managed.

Detailed responsibilities: - Define and communicate the Product Goal. -
Understand customer needs and business objectives. - Manage the Product
Backlog. - Make backlog items visible and understandable. - Order
backlog items according to value, risk, dependencies, and other needs. -
Clarify requirements and expected outcomes. - Make product-priority
trade-offs. - Collaborate with stakeholders. - Gather and consider
feedback. - Inspect product outcomes and adjust future priorities. -
Help the team understand why the work matters.

Product Owner in Scrum events: - Sprint Planning: explains product value
and priorities. - Daily Scrum: does not need to attend routinely; the
event is for Developers. - Sprint Review: discusses the outcome,
feedback, and future direction. - Sprint Retrospective: participates as
a member of the Scrum Team.

Example: The Product Owner has possible features such as viewing device
health, viewing active alerts, and changing dashboard colors. If users
urgently need to detect network failures, active alerts may be ordered
above cosmetic work.

The Product Owner does not simply assign every technical task to
individual Developers. The team collaborates, and Developers decide how
to do the work.

9.  SCRUM MASTER (SM)

Main responsibility: Establish Scrum as defined in the Scrum Guide and
help the Scrum Team become more effective.

The Scrum Master is a leader who serves the Scrum Team, the Product
Owner, and the organization.

Detailed responsibilities: - Teach and coach the team on Scrum. - Help
people understand Scrum values, events, artifacts, and commitments. -
Encourage self-management and cross-functional collaboration. - Help
remove impediments that slow or block progress. - Help Scrum events
remain useful and effective. - Support the Product Owner with backlog
management and stakeholder collaboration. - Promote transparency about
work, progress, and problems. - Encourage continuous improvement. - Help
the organization understand Scrum. - Work with others to remove
organizational barriers.

An impediment is anything that prevents or slows progress.

Examples: - A developer cannot access the test environment. - A required
software license is unavailable. - Another team has not provided a
needed API. - Requirements are unclear and block testing.

The Scrum Master helps address these obstacles. The Scrum Master is not
the only person who can solve a problem and is not simply a traditional
boss or task-assignment manager.

Scrum Master in Scrum events: - Sprint Planning: helps the event happen
effectively. - Daily Scrum: helps Developers understand its purpose and
keep it effective when needed. - Sprint Review: helps the event remain
collaborative and useful. - Sprint Retrospective: helps the team inspect
its working practices and plan improvements.

10. DEVELOPERS

Main responsibility: Create a usable Increment that meets the Definition
of Done during each Sprint.

Developers are the people doing the work needed to create the product.
Depending on the product, their skills may include programming, testing,
design, analysis, operations, security, and other areas.

Detailed responsibilities: - Create a plan for the Sprint work. - Decide
how to implement selected work. - Design and develop features. - Write
and maintain code when coding is part of the work. - Test features and
investigate defects. - Fix bugs. - Collaborate with teammates. -
Maintain quality according to the Definition of Done. - Inspect progress
toward the Sprint Goal. - Update the Sprint Backlog as the plan
changes. - Integrate completed work into the product. - Create a usable
Increment.

Example: For a device-health feature, Developers may clarify
requirements, design the API, implement the user interface, write tests,
fix bugs, and verify that the feature meets the Definition of Done.

Developers are collectively accountable for the Increment. Work may be
divided among individuals, but the team shares responsibility for
product quality.

11. STAKEHOLDERS

Stakeholders are people or groups who have an interest in the product or
are affected by it.

Examples: - Customers and end users. - Business managers. - Customer
support. - Operations teams. - Security and compliance teams. - Sales
and marketing teams.

How stakeholders contribute: - Explain business needs and user
problems. - Share expectations and constraints. - Provide feedback on
product outcomes. - Explain real-world usage. - Identify risks and
dependencies. - Communicate changing customer or market needs. -
Collaborate with the Product Owner on product direction.

Example: A network operations engineer says critical alerts must be easy
to identify during an outage. This feedback can help the Product Owner
and Developers improve how alerts are displayed.

Stakeholders provide feedback and information. They do not automatically
control the Sprint Backlog or assign tasks directly to Developers.

12. ROLE COMPARISON

Product Owner: - Focuses on product value and backlog priorities. - Is
accountable for effective Product Backlog management. - Explains
customer and business needs.

Scrum Master: - Focuses on Scrum effectiveness and team improvement. -
Coaches and supports the Scrum Team. - Helps address impediments.

Developers: - Focus on building a usable product Increment. - Plan and
adapt their work. - Ensure the Increment meets the Definition of Done.

Stakeholders: - Provide needs, expectations, context, and feedback. -
Help the team understand users and business conditions.

Easy way to remember: - Product Owner: What is valuable, and why? -
Scrum Master: How can the team use Scrum effectively? - Developers: How
will we build a usable Increment? - Stakeholders: What do users and the
business need?

============================================================ PART 4:
SCRUM EVENTS
============================================================

Scrum has five events: 1. Sprint. 2. Sprint Planning. 3. Daily Scrum. 4.
Sprint Review. 5. Sprint Retrospective.

13. SPRINT PLANNING

Purpose: Decide why the Sprint is valuable, what work can be completed,
and how the work will be done.

What happens: 1. Discuss why the Sprint is valuable. 2. Consider the
Product Backlog and select suitable work. 3. Establish the Sprint Goal.
4. Plan how the selected work can be delivered.

Example: Sprint Goal: “Provide basic visibility into network devices,
health, and alerts.” Possible selected stories: - View network devices:
2 story points. - View device health: 3 story points. - View active
alerts: 3 story points.

These numbers are examples only; story points are relative estimates.

Responsibilities: - Product Owner: explains value, priorities, and
requirements. - Developers: discuss what can be done and how to do it. -
Scrum Master: helps ensure the event happens and its purpose is
understood.

Output: The Sprint Backlog, including the Sprint Goal, selected Product
Backlog items, and a plan for delivering the work.

The Product Owner does not simply assign tasks. Developers plan how the
work will be completed.

14. DAILY SCRUM

Purpose: Inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the plan for
the next day’s work.

The Daily Scrum is a 15-minute event for Developers, held every working
day of the Sprint.

Developers may discuss: - Are we making progress toward the Sprint
Goal? - What should we focus on next? - Are there blockers? - Do we need
to adjust our plan?

Example: - Developer A: “The alert API is ready.” - Developer B: “The
alert display is in progress.” - Developer C: “I cannot test because the
test server is unavailable.”

The team coordinates next steps and works to address the blocker.

Important points: - It is 15 minutes. - It is held every working day of
the Sprint. - It is for coordination and adapting the plan, not a long
problem-solving meeting. - Detailed technical discussions can continue
afterward. - It is not merely a status report to a manager.

15. SPRINT REVIEW

Purpose: Inspect the Sprint outcome and determine useful next steps with
stakeholders.

What happens: 1. The team explains the Sprint Goal. 2. The team presents
or demonstrates the product outcome. 3. Stakeholders inspect the result
and provide feedback. 4. The participants discuss business needs and new
information. 5. The Product Backlog may be adjusted.

Example: The team demonstrates a network dashboard. A stakeholder asks
for a way to identify devices that have been offline for more than five
minutes. The Product Owner considers this feedback and may add or
reorder backlog items.

Responsibilities: - Developers explain and demonstrate the product
outcome. - Product Owner discusses product direction and priorities. -
Scrum Master helps the event remain useful and collaborative. -
Stakeholders inspect the outcome and provide feedback.

The Sprint Review focuses on the product and its future direction. It is
not just a formal presentation.

16. SPRINT RETROSPECTIVE

Purpose: Plan ways to improve quality and effectiveness.

The Scrum Team reviews how the Sprint went, including collaboration,
tools, processes, practices, and quality.

Questions: - What went well? - What did not go well? - What problems
occurred? - What could we do differently? - Which improvement should we
try next?

Example: Testing was delayed because requirements were unclear. The team
agrees to clarify acceptance criteria earlier and review test scenarios
before coding.

Responsibilities: - Developers share what helped or hindered delivery. -
Product Owner participates as a Scrum Team member. - Scrum Master helps
the team conduct the Retrospective effectively and supports
improvements.

Important points: - Focuses on how the team works. - Encourages honest
discussion without blaming people. - Should produce useful improvements,
not only complaints. - Improvements should be followed up in later
Sprints.

17. SPRINT REVIEW VERSUS RETROSPECTIVE

Sprint Review: - Focus: product outcome. - Discusses what was built and
learned. - Includes relevant stakeholders. - Feedback may affect product
priorities. - Example: “Users need a severity filter.”

Sprint Retrospective: - Focus: team effectiveness and quality. -
Discusses how the team worked. - Is for the Scrum Team. - Produces
improvements to working practices. - Example: “We should review test
cases earlier.”

Memory tip: Review = inspect the product and its future direction.
Retrospective = inspect the way the team works.

============================================================ PART 5:
SCRUM ARTIFACTS
============================================================

Scrum artifacts make work and progress visible. Each artifact has a
commitment that helps the team focus.

18. PRODUCT BACKLOG

The Product Backlog is an ordered, evolving list of work that may be
needed to improve the product.

It can include: - New features. - Improvements. - Bug fixes. - Technical
work. - Research or other work needed for the product.

Example Product Backlog for NetPulse: 1. View active alerts. 2. View
device health. 3. View network devices. 4. Acknowledge an alert. 5.
Filter alerts by severity. 6. View device metrics.

This is an illustrative order. Real priorities depend on current product
needs.

The Product Owner is accountable for effective Product Backlog
management. This includes making items visible and understandable and
ordering them appropriately. The Product Owner may ask Developers and
stakeholders to help clarify or refine items.

19. PRODUCT BACKLOG REFINEMENT

Refinement is an ongoing activity in which the team breaks down and
clarifies backlog items, adds details, and improves estimates where
useful.

Example: “Build a monitoring dashboard” may be too broad. It can be
broken into: - View network devices. - View device health. - View active
alerts. - Filter alerts by severity.

Refinement is an activity, not a separate formal Scrum event.

20. PRODUCT GOAL

The Product Goal is the long-term objective for the Scrum Team.

Example: “Provide operators with a reliable platform to monitor network
health and respond to issues.”

The Product Backlog evolves to help the team move toward this goal.

21. SPRINT BACKLOG

The Sprint Backlog contains: - The Sprint Goal. - Product Backlog items
selected for the Sprint. - The plan for delivering the work.

Example: Sprint Goal: “Provide basic visibility into network devices,
health, and alerts.”

Selected items: - View network devices: 2 points. - View device health:
3 points. - View active alerts: 3 points.

Developers may break items into tasks: - Design the API. - Implement the
endpoint. - Build the user interface. - Write tests. - Integrate the
feature.

Developers create and update the Sprint Backlog. It is not a fixed list
that can never change. The plan can be updated as the team learns more,
while the team continues to pursue the Sprint Goal.

22. SPRINT GOAL

The Sprint Goal is the single objective that gives the Sprint direction.

Example: “Provide basic visibility into network devices, health, and
active alerts.”

If a technical dependency makes one selected item harder than expected,
the team can discuss adjusting its plan and scope while protecting the
Sprint Goal.

23. INCREMENT

An Increment is a concrete, usable step toward the Product Goal that
meets the Definition of Done.

Example: If the product already displays network devices, a new
completed device-health feature can become part of the Increment once it
is integrated and meets the Definition of Done.

Important points: - The Increment must be usable. - It must meet the
Definition of Done. - It should integrate with previous Increments. -
Multiple Increments can be created during one Sprint. - The Increment
does not necessarily have to be publicly released immediately.

24. THE THREE ARTIFACTS AND THEIR COMMITMENTS

Product Backlog -> Product Goal: The long-term objective for the
product.

Sprint Backlog -> Sprint Goal: The objective for the current Sprint.

Increment -> Definition of Done: The quality standard that work must
meet to be considered complete.

25. PRODUCT BACKLOG VERSUS SPRINT BACKLOG

Product Backlog: - Contains potential work for the product. - Supports
the Product Goal. - Is ordered by the Product Owner. - Can include work
for many future Sprints. - Evolves as product needs change.

Sprint Backlog: - Contains the current Sprint Goal, selected items, and
delivery plan. - Supports the Sprint Goal. - Is created and managed by
Developers. - Focuses on the current Sprint. - Changes as Developers
learn more during the Sprint.

============================================================ PART 6:
USER STORIES, EPICS, STORY POINTS, AND ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA
============================================================

26. USER STORY

A user story is a short description of a feature from the user’s
perspective.

Common format: “As a [type of user], I want [a feature or capability],
so that [a benefit or reason].”

Example: “As a network operator, I want to view active alerts so that I
can quickly identify network problems.”

Parts of the story: - “As a network operator” = who needs it. - “I want
to view active alerts” = what is needed. - “So that I can quickly
identify network problems” = why it is useful.

Why use user stories? - They explain requirements in a user-focused
way. - They help the team understand the value of a feature. - They
encourage discussion. - They organize work into manageable pieces. -
They provide a starting point for acceptance criteria and testing.

The Product Owner is accountable for Product Backlog management, but
user stories can be written and refined collaboratively by the Product
Owner, Developers, and stakeholders. A user story is a common Agile
technique, not a format required by Scrum itself.

27. EPIC

An epic is a large piece of work that can be broken into smaller
stories.

Example epic: “Monitor network devices.”

Possible stories: 1. As an operator, I want to view all network devices.
2. As an operator, I want to see the health of each device. 3. As an
operator, I want to view active alerts. 4. As an operator, I want to
filter alerts by severity. 5. As an operator, I want to view device
metrics.

Epic versus user story: - Epic: a large requirement or feature area. -
User story: a smaller, specific user need. - An epic may contain several
related stories. - A story may be small enough to estimate and implement
within a Sprint.

An epic is a commonly used backlog concept, not a separate formal Scrum
artifact.

28. STORY POINTS

Story points estimate the relative size of work. They may account for
effort, complexity, and uncertainty. They are not a direct measurement
of time.

Example: - View network devices: 2 points. - View device health: 3
points. - Integrate complex alert processing: 8 points.

These numbers are examples. Each team estimates work according to its
context.

When estimating, the team may consider: - Effort: how much work may be
involved. - Complexity: how difficult the problem is. - Uncertainty: how
much is unknown. - Dependencies: whether the work relies on other
systems or teams.

Some teams use Fibonacci-style scales such as 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, and 13.
Larger numbers represent work with greater relative size or uncertainty.

Why use story points? - Compare the relative size of backlog items. -
Support planning discussions. - Identify stories that may be too
large. - Learn from previous delivery patterns.

29. VELOCITY

Velocity is the amount of work a team completes in a Sprint, often
measured in story points if that team uses story-point estimation.

Example: - Sprint 1: 18 completed points. - Sprint 2: 20 completed
points. - Sprint 3: 19 completed points.

The team may use its past results to inform future planning. Velocity is
a planning aid, not a measure of individual productivity or a target
that should be manipulated. Story points are team-specific, so comparing
velocity across teams is usually misleading.

Story points versus hours: - Story points estimate relative size and
uncertainty. - Hours estimate time. - Story points do not have a
universal conversion to hours. - Different teams may use different
story-point scales.

30. ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA

Acceptance criteria are specific conditions that a user story or feature
must satisfy to meet the agreed requirements.

Example user story: “As a user, I want to log in so that I can access my
account.”

Possible acceptance criteria: 1. The user can enter a registered email
address and password. 2. Valid credentials allow the user to log in. 3.
Invalid credentials produce a suitable error message. 4. The password is
not displayed as plain text while being entered. 5. An unauthenticated
user cannot access protected account pages.

These are examples. Actual criteria should reflect the product’s
security and business requirements.

How acceptance criteria help: - Clarify what a feature must do. - Help
Developers understand expected behavior. - Help testers create test
cases. - Reduce misunderstandings. - Help verify whether the story meets
its requirements.

Example test cases: - Enter a registered email and correct password;
verify login succeeds. - Enter an incorrect password; verify a suitable
error appears. - Type a password; verify it is masked. - Open a
protected account page without logging in; verify access is denied.

31. ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA VERSUS DEFINITION OF DONE

Acceptance criteria: - Specific to a particular feature or backlog
item. - Describe the required behavior. - Example: invalid password
displays an error.

Definition of Done: - A shared quality standard for the Increment. -
Describes the quality state required for completion. - Example: required
automated tests pass.

A feature may satisfy its acceptance criteria but still fail the
Definition of Done if required quality checks have not been completed.

32. DEFINITION OF DONE (DoD)

The Definition of Done is the shared formal description of the state an
Increment must meet to satisfy the required quality measures.

It helps everyone understand what “done” means. Without it, one person
may consider coding complete while another expects tests, reviews, and
integration.

Example Definition of Done: - The feature is implemented. - Required
code review is completed. - Required automated tests pass. - Acceptance
criteria are satisfied. - The change is integrated with the product. -
Required security and quality checks pass. - The feature is usable and
meets the agreed quality standard.

The exact Definition of Done depends on the product, organization, and
applicable standards. The list above is an example, not a universal
checklist.

The Scrum Team is responsible for creating an Increment that meets the
Definition of Done. Developers follow the Definition of Done while
creating the Increment. If several Scrum Teams work on the same product,
they must follow the same Definition of Done.

If an item does not meet the Definition of Done, it is not part of the
Increment. It returns to the Product Backlog for future consideration.

============================================================ PART 7: HOW
ALL THE CONCEPTS WORK TOGETHER
============================================================

33. COMPLETE SCRUM WORKFLOW

Example product: NetPulse, a network-monitoring application.

Step 1: Define the Product Goal. The product should help network
operators monitor devices and respond to network problems.

Step 2: Create the Product Backlog. List potential features such as
device health, active alerts, and device metrics.

Step 3: Refine backlog items. Clarify requirements, break large items
into smaller items, and estimate them when useful.

Step 4: Conduct Sprint Planning. Choose a Sprint Goal, select suitable
work, and plan how to deliver it.

Step 5: Work during the Sprint. Developers implement and test features.
They hold the Daily Scrum each working day and adapt their plan.

Step 6: Check the Definition of Done. Confirm that completed work meets
the team’s agreed quality standards.

Step 7: Create an Increment. Integrate usable work that meets the
Definition of Done.

Step 8: Conduct the Sprint Review. Inspect the product outcome with
stakeholders and discuss feedback and possible next steps.

Step 9: Conduct the Sprint Retrospective. Discuss how the team worked
and identify improvements.

Step 10: Start the next Sprint. Use the updated Product Backlog,
feedback, and lessons learned to continue improving the product.

The Daily Scrum happens throughout the Sprint, not only after
development is complete.

34. SCRUM BOARD

A Scrum board is a visual way to track work during a Sprint. Teams may
use a physical board or a tool such as Jira.

A simple board can contain: - To Do: work that has not started. - In
Progress: work currently being performed. - Done: work that satisfies
the team’s completion criteria.

Example: To Do: - View device health. - View active alerts.

In Progress: - View network devices.

Done: - Completed and verified work.

Teams may use different columns. Moving a card to Done does not
automatically prove that it meets the Definition of Done; the agreed
quality standards must still be followed.

35. AGILE VERSUS SCRUM

Agile: - A broad mindset based on values and principles. - Includes
several approaches and methods. - Encourages feedback, collaboration,
and adaptation. - Does not prescribe one single workflow for all teams.

Scrum: - A specific framework. - Defines accountabilities, events,
artifacts, and commitments. - Uses Sprints to organize work and
learning. - Provides a structure for inspection and adaptation.

Other Agile approaches include Kanban and Extreme Programming (XP).
Scrum teams may also use engineering practices from other approaches.

============================================================ PART 8:
IMPORTANT DIFFERENCES
============================================================

Agile versus Scrum: Agile is a broad approach and set of values; Scrum
is a specific framework.

Sprint versus iteration: A Sprint is a formal Scrum concept; iteration
is a general development-cycle term.

Epic versus user story: An epic is a large requirement; a user story is
a smaller user-focused need.

Product Backlog versus Sprint Backlog: The Product Backlog contains
potential product work; the Sprint Backlog contains the Sprint Goal,
selected items, and the plan for the current Sprint.

Product Goal versus Sprint Goal: The Product Goal is the long-term
product objective; the Sprint Goal is the objective for the current
Sprint.

Acceptance criteria versus Definition of Done: Acceptance criteria
describe requirements for a specific item; the Definition of Done
describes the shared quality standard for the Increment.

Sprint Review versus Sprint Retrospective: The Review inspects the
product outcome and future direction; the Retrospective improves the
team’s way of working.

Product Owner versus Scrum Master: The Product Owner focuses on product
value and backlog management; the Scrum Master focuses on Scrum
effectiveness and team improvement.

Scrum Master versus Developers: The Scrum Master coaches and supports
Scrum effectiveness; Developers create the product Increment.

Story points versus hours: Story points estimate relative size; hours
estimate time.

Increment versus unfinished work: An Increment is usable and meets the
Definition of Done; unfinished work that does not meet it is not part of
the Increment.

============================================================ PART 9:
QUICK REVISION
============================================================

Agile: Develop software in small steps, get feedback, and improve
continuously.

Scrum: A framework for working in Sprints to deliver value.

Sprint: A fixed period of one month or less with a Sprint Goal.

Iteration: A repeated cycle of development work.

User story: A short description of a user need and its benefit.

Epic: A large requirement that can be broken into smaller stories.

Story point: An estimate of relative work size, complexity, and
uncertainty.

Acceptance criteria: Conditions a particular feature must satisfy.

Product Owner: Maximizes product value and is accountable for effective
Product Backlog management.

Scrum Master: Helps the team and organization use Scrum effectively.

Developers: Create a usable Increment that meets the Definition of Done.

Stakeholders: Provide business context, needs, and feedback.

Sprint Planning: Plans why the Sprint is valuable, what can be done, and
how.

Daily Scrum: A 15-minute event for Developers to inspect progress and
adapt their plan.

Sprint Review: Inspects the product outcome with stakeholders and
considers next steps.

Sprint Retrospective: Identifies improvements to the team’s quality and
effectiveness.

Product Backlog: Ordered, evolving list of potential product work.

Sprint Backlog: Sprint Goal, selected Product Backlog items, and
delivery plan.

Increment: A usable step toward the Product Goal that meets the
Definition of Done.

Sprint Goal: The objective that gives the current Sprint direction.

Product Goal: The long-term objective for the product.

Definition of Done: The shared quality standard an Increment must meet.

============================================================ PART 10:
SAMPLE INTERVIEW ANSWER
============================================================

Question: “Explain Agile and Scrum in simple words.”

Answer: “Agile is an approach to software development that focuses on
delivering value in small steps, collecting feedback regularly, and
adapting to change. Scrum is an Agile framework that organizes work into
Sprints.

The Product Owner focuses on product value and backlog priorities. The
Scrum Master helps the team use Scrum effectively and improve.
Developers build and test the product and create a usable Increment that
meets the Definition of Done.

Sprint Planning establishes the Sprint Goal and plans the work. The
Daily Scrum helps Developers coordinate. The Sprint Review inspects the
product outcome with stakeholders, and the Sprint Retrospective helps
the team improve its way of working.

The Product Backlog contains potential product work. The Sprint Backlog
contains the current Sprint Goal, selected items, and the plan.
Acceptance criteria describe what a particular feature must do, while
the Definition of Done describes the shared quality standard for
completed work.”
